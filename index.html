HCI RESEARCH PROJECT · XJTLU

Evaluating eHall AI
at XJTLU

Assessing how AI-supported campus information retrieval affects task efficiency, answer quality, trust, satisfaction, and students’ ability to verify and recover from incorrect information.

Explore the study View the procedure

Target group: 92 first-year postgraduate students in AIAT Class 1. Participation and final sample size will depend on voluntary recruitment.

01 · RESEARCH MOTIVATION

Why evaluate eHall AI?

XJTLU’s eHall is an integrated online portal for university information, services, applications, schedules, tasks, search, and other campus resources. Its AI Q&A function provides a natural-language route for students seeking answers, service links, or explanations of administrative procedures.

The study compares traditional information retrieval with AI-assisted retrieval. It examines not only whether AI helps students find information faster, but also whether the answers are accurate, complete, verifiable, and aligned with students’ goals and expectations.

MICRO LEVEL

Task performance

Measure completion time, success rate, answer correctness, and error recovery during specific campus-information tasks.

USER EXPERIENCE

Trust and satisfaction

Understand perceived efficiency, ease of use, confidence, satisfaction, and willingness to use eHall AI again.

MACRO LEVEL

Campus service needs

Explore students’ common information needs, concerns about privacy and reliability, and priorities for future improvement.

Important platform check

The exact capabilities, interface, source citations, and access restrictions of the logged-in eHall AI page must be checked before the experiment. Tasks should be based on services and information that are confirmed to be available; unverified platform features must not be assumed.

02 · RESEARCH QUESTIONS

What do we want to find out?

RQ1 · EFFICIENCY

Does AI save time?

Is eHall AI faster than traditional non-AI information retrieval for comparable campus tasks?

Indicators: completion time, completion rate, first-attempt success.

RQ2 · INFORMATION QUALITY

Can users detect errors?

How accurate, complete, and verifiable are AI answers, and can students identify and correct errors?

Indicators: answer accuracy, error detection, error correction, erroneous trust.

RQ3 · EXPERIENCE

Is AI more satisfying?

Does AI improve perceived ease of use, satisfaction, and future use intention, and does it meet expectations?

RQ4 · USER GOALS

How do students interact?

What are students trying to achieve, what help do they expect, and how do they query, verify, follow up, or seek human help?

RQ5 · SERVICE VALUE

Does it meet student needs?

What are the class’s overall attitudes, common use cases, trust concerns, and priorities for improvement?

HYPOTHESES

Test, do not assume

We expect AI may reduce task time and improve perceived efficiency. Effects on accuracy, satisfaction, trust, and task type will be tested rather than treated as established findings.

04 · PILOT STUDY DESIGN

A small-scale rehearsal before the main study

This pilot compares eHall AI-assisted retrieval with traditional official-information retrieval for campus tasks at XJTLU's SIP (Suzhou Industrial Park) campus. It will check task difficulty, session flow, and recording procedures while collecting genuine preliminary evidence on efficiency, answer correctness, user experience, and natural verification behaviour.

4tasks per participant
30–35 minplanned session duration
Up to 32task records if all take part

Planned protocol, not findings. This section describes the current eight-person pilot. The later sections retain the broader proposed main-study framework; its additional measures, task examples, and longer interviews are not automatically part of this pilot.

PARTICIPANTS & RECRUITMENT

Eight SIP campus postgraduates

Recruit eight current SIP campus postgraduate students voluntarily, with varied prior eHall experience. If the original AIAT first-year Class 1 population remains the target, recruit eligible SIP students within that class; report any expansion of the recruitment scope.

  • Do not ask participants to learn the tasks in advance.
  • Participation does not affect grades or university services; the study evaluates retrieval methods, not individual ability.
  • Research-team rehearsal data does not count towards the eight participants.
PREPARATION & CONSISTENCY

Verify materials before recruitment

Check the four official answers, retain sources, verification dates, and page copies, and rehearse both retrieval methods. Keep task wording and scoring rules consistent throughout this pilot.

  • Prepare information sheets, consent forms, questionnaires, answer forms, and researcher logs.
  • Standardise the computer, browser, and network where feasible; complete required login before timing.
  • Define storage, access, retention, and withdrawal arrangements and complete the required course research procedures.
  • Obtain separate screen-recording consent; use manual observation if recording is declined.

Two retrieval conditions

CONDITION N · TRADITIONAL RETRIEVAL

Official sources without AI

Use official university websites, service guidance, and on-site search. Ordinary search engines may be used to locate official pages. Generative answers in search results, other chatbots, and help from other people are not allowed.

CONDITION AI · EHALL AI-ASSISTED RETRIEVAL

Ask eHall AI first

Begin every task with a question to eHall AI. Follow-up questions and checking official webpages are allowed. Other AI tools and help from other people are not allowed. Use a fresh AI conversation for each task.

Both conditions have access to the same scope of official information. AI-condition time includes response generation, follow-up questions, and webpage checks. All tasks require written answers only: participants do not actually request certificates, reserve rooms, or make phone calls.

Two task sets, four concrete campus queries

Task limits are starting values for the pilot. Any changes identified during internal rehearsal must be applied consistently before recruiting participants.

Session flow · approximately 30–35 minutes

~3 min

1. Information and consent

Explain voluntary participation, the right to stop, data handling, and that answer correctness does not affect personal assessment.

~2 min

2. Background questionnaire

Record SIP postgraduate and AIAT Class 1 eligibility, previous eHall AI use and use days in the past 30 days, other generative AI use, familiarity with official campus-information sources (1–5), prior experience of the four services, and usual first-choice retrieval method. Do not request names, student IDs, credentials, or remembered service answers.

~2 min

3. Familiarisation

Explain the answer form, timer, and submission process using an unrelated practice task. Do not reveal answers to the study tasks.

Up to 8 min

4. First task set

Present one task at a time under the assigned condition. After each final submission, collect an answer-confidence rating outside the task timer.

~1 min

5. First condition questionnaire

Collect the four experience ratings for the method just used, referring only to that round's two tasks.

~1 min

6. Break and switch

Close previous task pages and answers and clear the search state. Switch to the other method and the other task set.

Up to 9 min

7. Second task set and questionnaire

Complete the other two tasks, collect confidence after each task, and repeat the same four experience items for the second condition.

~4–5 min

8. Short interview and debrief

Ask about difficulty, trust, verification, method preference, and desired improvements. Provide the correct answers and research contact information. Confirm actual session duration during the pilot.

Consistent moderation and timing

“Please use the method assigned to this stage to complete the task on screen. Search in your usual way and submit when you think you have enough information; you may note anything you are unsure about. There is a time limit. When time is up, submit whatever you have.”

Start timing when the full task is visible and work is permitted; stop at the single final submission. Include normal page loading and AI response time. Record actual time and status for abandonment; at the limit, collect the available answer and mark it as timed out. Log technical failures and their duration without silently deleting or adjusting records. Moderators explain operating rules only; any accidental content hints must be recorded.

Measures, scoring, and observation

PER TASK · OBJECTIVE OUTCOMES

Time and content score

Record elapsed seconds and score the answer against three verified points: one point each, for a total of 0–3. Full success means all three points are correct within the time limit. Retain partial scores; unresolved contradictory answers earn no credit for that point. Verified equivalent wording and valid official alternative contact details may receive credit.

PER TASK · CONFIDENCE

One rating after submission

“How confident are you that the answer you just submitted is correct?” Use 1 = Not at all confident to 5 = Very confident. Collect this after timing stops and keep perceived correctness separate from independently scored correctness.

OBSERVED BEHAVIOUR

Opening is not verifying

Record whether an official source was opened and, separately, whether official information was explicitly used to check an existing answer. Mark unclear verification as “Cannot determine”. Do not remind participants to verify before every task; ask verification questions after all tasks are finished.

The same four items after each condition

Rate each statement from 1 = Strongly disagree to 5 = Strongly agree. Report items separately; do not label their sum as a validated usability score.
AspectStatement
Perceived efficiencyThis method helped me find the information I needed efficiently.
ClarityThe information obtained through this method was easy to understand.
Allow “Not applicable: no information was obtained that could be evaluated for clarity”.
SatisfactionOverall, I am satisfied with this method.
Future useI would use this method for similar campus-information tasks in the future.
TASK-LEVEL RECORD

One row per attempted task

Log anonymous ID, allocation sequence, stage, task ID, condition, time, outcome status (submitted, timed out, abandoned, or technical exception), verbatim answer, content score, confidence, source-opening and verification observations, AI follow-up count where applicable, and moderator assistance or exceptions.

Retain de-identified AI prompts and responses to distinguish output errors from interpretation errors. Two researchers should independently score all available answers, ideally with condition and timing hidden; preserve initial scores before resolving differences.

POST-TASK INTERVIEW

Neutral prompts, specific examples

  • Which task was hardest, and where did you get stuck?
  • How did you judge whether the information was trustworthy?
  • When did you check official sources, and why?
  • Which method better suited these tasks? Can you give an example?
  • If you could change one feature or step, what would you change?

Avoid leading questions that assume AI was faster or more trustworthy.

Analysis and decisions for the next study

01 · WITHIN-PERSON SUMMARY

Compare paired outcomes

For each participant and condition, summarise time across two tasks, total content score (0–6), number of fully successful tasks (0–2), and each experience item. Show individual paired results alongside medians, ranges, and actual success counts.

02 · DESCRIPTIVE INTERPRETATION

Check feasibility and imbalance

Inspect each task under both conditions for difficulty differences. Label timeouts, abandonment, and technical exceptions; do not present their times as successful completion times. Eight participants are the sample, not 32 independent participants. Statistical significance is not the primary aim.

03 · PILOT DELIVERABLES

Turn observations into revisions

Report allocation and actual completion, up to 32 real task records, paired performance and experience, anonymised interview excerpts, and an “Issue → Impact on the study → Proposed revision” log. Date and version any mid-pilot changes, and explain differences before combining data.

Error verification: observe naturally in this round

The additional SIP-versus-Taicang Academic Affairs Office verification question is excluded because A1 may reveal the answer in advance, confounding verification with memory. Observe natural checking and error handling within the four main tasks instead. Any later independent verification task should use a new service, have its own verified answer key, and appear last.

Public reporting should contain aggregate results and anonymised excerpts. Keep consent records, personal details, and complete screen recordings private. Preliminary observations must come from collected data; do not present anticipated AI benefits as findings.

05 · EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN

Within-subject comparison: No AI vs With AI

Each participant experiences both information-retrieval conditions, allowing each person to serve as their own comparison. To reduce learning and carry-over effects, participants complete parallel task sets of comparable difficulty rather than repeating exactly the same questions.

CONDITION A

No AI

Participants use traditional search, official university webpages, and service pages to locate and evaluate information. They do not use the AI Q&A function.

CONDITION B

With AI

Participants use eHall AI to answer the task and may ask follow-up questions or check official sources within the study rules.

Counterbalancing and task sets

GroupStage 1Stage 2
AB groupNo AI + Task Set AWith AI + Task Set B
BA groupWith AI + Task Set ANo AI + Task Set B

Randomly assign participants to AB or BA, aiming for similar group sizes and stratifying by programme where feasible. Task Sets A and B should cover the same task types at similar difficulty. Pilot testing should be used to adjust task difficulty.

Controlled comparison

Keep task difficulty, answer rubrics, devices, browser, network environment, instructions, time limits, access procedures, and scoring standards as consistent as possible. Record the eHall AI version and any platform or knowledge-base changes during data collection.

07 · TASK DESIGN & ERROR EVALUATION

Realistic, verifiable campus tasks

Each task should have a clear goal, observable actions, and a defensible answer key. The examples below are templates; before the study, the team must confirm the relevant service and official information are currently accessible.

TASK 01

Fact finding

Goal: Find an official service entry point, responsible department, or eligibility condition.

Expected help: A concise and direct answer.

Scoring: Factual correctness, valid source, completion time.

TASK 02

Procedural understanding

Goal: Summarise the steps, required materials, and channel for a verified campus service.

Expected help: Organise dispersed information into clear steps.

Scoring: Completeness and correctness of steps and conditions.

TASK 03

Information synthesis

Goal: Compare details across two official sources and draw a supported conclusion.

Expected help: Summarise key information and make sources traceable.

Scoring: Coverage, conclusion accuracy, source traceability.

TASK 04

Error detection and verification

Goal: Check an answer against authoritative information, identify inaccuracies, and provide a correction.

Expected help: Correct information or a clear statement of uncertainty.

Scoring: Error detection, error correction, and erroneous trust.

USER INTENTION

What are you trying to do?

Before each task, ask what the participant intends to achieve, what help they expect, and what outcome would count as success.

OUTCOME

Did the user succeed?

Record actions, time, final outcome, verification behaviour, and whether the participant accepted incorrect information.

Fair and safe error testing

Build an answer key from current official sources before testing, recording sources and update dates. At least two researchers should independently check key answers. Observe naturally occurring AI errors; if controlled error examples are needed, use clearly labelled research materials in a simulated task. Do not alter the live university system or expose students to false information for real administrative decisions.

Error categories

TypeHow it will be checked
Factual errorCompare service names, eligibility, and other facts with official sources.
Procedural errorCheck whether required steps are missing or out of order.
Outdated informationCheck dates and whether the referenced rule is still valid.
Source/link errorOpen links and confirm that they support the answer.
OmissionUse the scoring rubric to identify missing critical conditions.
Unjustified certaintyAssess whether the answer states uncertain claims as definite facts.

10 · INTERVIEW GUIDE

Understand why participants behaved as they did

SECTION 01

Goals and expectations

What campus information do you usually need? What did you expect eHall AI to do? Did the experience meet those expectations?

SECTION 02

AI vs traditional search

Which method was more efficient or easier to understand? When did traditional search work better? What extra steps did AI require?

SECTION 03

Accuracy and trust

Did you encounter incorrect or unclear information? How did you judge trustworthiness? Did you check official sources or ask follow-up questions?

SECTION 04

Satisfaction and future use

11 · ETHICS & PRIVACY

Voluntary participation and responsible data handling

Voluntary participation

Participants may decline or stop without penalty and without impact on grades, relationships with staff, or access to university services. Recruitment should avoid pressure from classmates or anyone with assessment authority.

Data minimisation

Use anonymous participant IDs. Avoid collecting names, student IDs, account credentials, private conversations, or other unnecessary personal data.

Secure storage

Limit access to authorised research team members. Before data collection, specify storage location, retention period, deletion procedure, withdrawal deadline, and contact details.

AI answer limitations

AI answers may be inaccurate or incomplete. They are research materials, not authoritative guidance for real administrative decisions; official university sources take precedence.

Recording consent

Screen activity or interview audio should be recorded only after the participant receives an explanation and gives separate consent where required.

Ethical review

Complete applicable university ethics and data-protection procedures before implementation. Do not claim approval has been granted until it has been confirmed.

The consent form should state the study purpose, activities, estimated duration, risks, data collection and use, withdrawal conditions, researcher contact, and optional consent for audio or screen recording. Results should be reported using aggregated statistics and anonymised quotations.
