HCI RESEARCH PROJECT · XJTLU

Evaluating eHall AI
at XJTLU

Assessing how AI-supported campus information retrieval affects task efficiency, answer quality, and user experience.

Explore the study View the procedure

Target group: 92 first-year postgraduate students in AIAT Class 1. Participation and final sample size will depend on voluntary recruitment.

01 · RESEARCH MOTIVATION

Why evaluate eHall AI?

XJTLU’s eHall is an integrated online portal for university information, services, applications, schedules, tasks, search, and other campus resources. Its AI Q&A function provides a natural-language route for students seeking answers, service links, or explanations of administrative procedures.

The study compares traditional information retrieval with AI-assisted retrieval. It examines not only whether AI helps students find information faster, but also whether the answers are accurate, complete, verifiable, and aligned with students’ goals and expectations.

MICRO LEVEL

Task performance

Measure completion time, success rate, answer correctness, and error recovery during specific campus-information tasks.

USER EXPERIENCE

Trust and satisfaction

Understand perceived efficiency, ease of use, confidence, satisfaction, and willingness to use eHall AI again.

MACRO LEVEL

Campus service needs

Explore students’ common information needs, concerns about privacy and reliability, and priorities for future improvement.

Important platform check

The exact capabilities, interface, source citations, and access restrictions of the logged-in eHall AI page must be checked before the experiment. Tasks should be based on services and information that are confirmed to be available; unverified platform features must not be assumed.

02 · RESEARCH QUESTIONS

What do we want to find out?

RQ1 · EFFICIENCY

Does AI save time?

Is eHall AI faster than traditional non-AI information retrieval for comparable campus tasks?

Indicators: completion time, completion rate, first-attempt success.

RQ2 · INFORMATION QUALITY

Can users detect errors?

How accurate, complete, and verifiable are AI answers, and can students identify and correct errors?

Indicators: answer accuracy, error detection, error correction, erroneous trust.

RQ3 · EXPERIENCE

Is AI more satisfying?

Does AI improve perceived ease of use, satisfaction, and future use intention, and does it meet expectations?

RQ4 · USER GOALS

How do students interact?

What are students trying to achieve, what help do they expect, and how do they query, verify, follow up, or seek human help?

RQ5 · SERVICE VALUE

Does it meet student needs?

What are the class’s overall attitudes, common use cases, trust concerns, and priorities for improvement?

HYPOTHESES

Test, do not assume

We expect AI may reduce task time and improve perceived efficiency. Effects on accuracy, satisfaction, trust, and task type will be tested rather than treated as established findings.

04 · PILOT STUDY DESIGN

A small-scale test before the main study

This pilot compares eHall AI-assisted retrieval with traditional official-information search at XJTLU’s SIP campus. It checks task difficulty, session flow, and data-recording procedures while collecting preliminary evidence on efficiency, answer correctness, user experience, and natural verification behaviour.

Scope: this section presents the pilot design followed by its experimental results. The subsequent sections present the separate main-study protocol and the reported results for 16 participants. These main-study analyses are not part of the eight-person pilot.

8 VOLUNTEERS

AIAT First-Year Class 1

We randomly selected eight volunteers from AIAT First-Year Class 1 to participate in the pilot study.

30–35 MINUTES

One session, both methods

Each person completes four different tasks: two with each method. Participation is voluntary and does not affect grades or university services. The study evaluates the retrieval method, not individual ability.

UP TO 32 TASK RECORDS

Preliminary evidence

Record time, content scores, confidence, verification, and experience. Use the findings primarily to improve the protocol, rather than to establish statistical significance or claim that AI is superior.

CONDITION N

Traditional retrieval

Use official university websites, service guidance, and site search. Ordinary search engines may locate official pages, but generated AI summaries, other chatbots, and help from other people are not allowed.

CONDITION AI

eHall AI-assisted retrieval

Start every task by asking eHall AI. Follow-up questions and verification on official webpages are allowed. Other AI tools and help from other people are not allowed. Start a new AI conversation for each task.

Both conditions access the same scope of official information. AI response waiting, follow-up questions, and webpage verification all count towards task time. Participants only look up information and submit written answers: they do not apply for certificates, book rooms, or make phone calls.

Each set contains one fact-finding task followed by one rules/procedure task, with a maximum of eight minutes per set.

Before the first participant

Verify all official answers and archive sources, checking dates, and page copies. Keep task wording and scoring rules consistent throughout the pilot. Rehearse both methods; prepare the information sheet, consent form, background and post-condition questionnaires, answer sheets, and researcher logs. Standardise the computer, browser, and network as far as possible; complete logins before timing starts.

Specify data storage, authorised access, retention, and the withdrawal deadline, and complete the required course research procedures. Close private pages. Obtain separate consent for screen recording; use manual observation if recording is declined.

STANDARDISED TIMING

Time the complete search

Start when the full task is visible and interaction is allowed; stop at the single final answer submission. Include normal page loading and AI waiting. Record actual elapsed time for early abandonment; at the limit, collect the existing answer and mark a timeout.

Log network failures, crashes, durations, and any accidental content hints. The moderator explains operation only and does not suggest keywords, departments, sources, or answers. Never silently remove or adjust technical incidents.

ANSWER SCORING

Three points per task

Award one point per correct predefined element: 3 = fully correct, 1–2 = partially correct, and 0 = no correct elements. Full success means all three points within the time limit. Retain partial scores.

Contradictory alternatives earn no point unless the correct answer is explicitly selected. Verified equivalent wording and valid official alternative contact details may count.

What the charts show

eHall AI was faster and scored higher on A1 and B2. Traditional search performed better on B1, while A2 showed similar response times and a slightly higher score with traditional search. AI’s advantage therefore depended on the task. More positive experience ratings did not always correspond to better answer quality.

Confidence, source checking, and interview insights

CONFIDENCE & CHECKING

High confidence, limited source access

Mean AI answer confidence was 4.12/5, while the achieved score rate was about 71%. These are different measures, but high confidence in incomplete answers suggests possible over-trust. An official source was opened in 8 of 16 AI tasks; opening a page does not necessarily mean the answer was verified.

FOUR INTERVIEWS

Clearer routes and checkable answers

Three interviewees identified A1 as the hardest task because official entry points were unclear. Specific answers often felt credible, and checking was usually prompted by uncertainty. Participants requested direct source links, more complete answers, faster responses, and a frequently asked questions list.

Limitations and improvements for the main study

  • Small sample: eight participants from one class and four interviews limit generalisability. Broaden interview coverage in the main study.
  • Time limits: 13 of 32 responses were capped (AI: 4; traditional: 9). These are recorded response times, not known completion times; interpret timing comparisons cautiously.
  • Consistent records: actual task rounds were not retained and questionnaire timing varied. Record actual round order and administer the questionnaire once after each round.
  • Better verification measures: separately record official-page opening, explicit answer checking, and AI follow-up counts. Retain B1 and check equivalent official contact channels when refining the scoring rubric.

Main-study design

A within-subject crossover study compared eHall AI with traditional official-website search. Each participant completed four tasks: one fact-finding and one rule/procedure task under each condition, using different task sets to reduce repeated-answer learning.

Completed sample: 16 SIP-campus postgraduate students from AIAT Year 1, Class 1, yielding 64 task records and 16 paired observations. Protocol target: 24–32 participants. The reported analyses use the actual sample of 16; the planned recruitment target was not reached.

Two retrieval conditions

Traditional search: retrieve information through official university websites and eHall service pages without the AI Q&A function. eHall AI: use the assistant to answer the task, with follow-up questions and official-source verification available.

The protocol specifies block randomisation: shuffle four allocation cards, one per sequence, within each block. The analysis report states that condition and task-set order were counterbalanced but does not provide realised sequence counts. Within each set, the fact-finding task always precedes the rule/procedure task; task-type effects cannot be isolated from this fixed order.

The protocol excludes the eight pilot participants and calibration pretest participants from the main sample. Devices, network, instructions, answer rubrics and time limits should be standardised across conditions.

Scoring and exceptions

The protocol requires two independent blinded scorers, reconciliation of disagreements, and a frozen answer key checked against official sources. Inter-rater agreement was planned, but no agreement coefficient is supplied in the statistical report. A task made unevaluable by a technical failure should receive a missing score, not zero; interruptions and their duration should be logged. The supplied report states that the analysed records contained no missing values.

Difficulty calibration in the protocol

Match task sets on structure, information depth, cognitive demand, prior exposure and eHall AI answerability. Official answers should be reachable in approximately 2–3 navigation levels. Two researchers independently verify the answer key and retain URLs, screenshots and verification dates.

The planned calibration includes timed researcher trials and a separate 2–4-person pretest. Researcher completion should stay within 60% of each time limit; pretest full-success rates should be approximately 30–80%, without marked difficulty gaps. Replace unsuitable tasks before changing individual time limits. Calibration outcomes are not supplied in the statistical report.

Timing and reset rules

Stop each task at its limit and record a timeout; no additional answer time is allowed. Waiting for AI output or page loading remains part of recorded time. Between participants, close tabs, end AI sessions, clear downloads and the clipboard, reset questionnaires and archive records. The protocol proposes two matched workstations and standardised facilitator scripts.

Main-study results

Lower recorded time with eHall AI. Median total time across two tasks was 202 seconds with AI versus 334 seconds with traditional search: a 132-second difference between condition medians, or approximately 39.5% lower. This is not the median of individual paired differences.

Data preparation and analysis

The supplied SPSS analysis report cross-checked 64 task records and 64 experience questionnaires and reports no missing values or recorded times beyond the task limits. Fifteen times were nevertheless capped at their limits: 13 traditional-search tasks and 2 AI tasks. Analysis aggregated the four task records from each participant into 16 paired observations.

Two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used for eight outcomes (α = .05), given the small sample and ordinal ratings. A paired-samples t-test provided a supplementary time analysis. Both analyses use the recorded, time-limited outcomes; neither removes the censoring limitation.

Interpretation and limitations

The strongest evidence is a reduction in recorded retrieval time (r = .70). No statistically significant differences were detected in correctness, full-score tasks, confidence or the four experience ratings. Non-significant results do not establish equivalence, demonstrate no accuracy cost, or prove equal user experience.

Official sources were opened in fewer than one third of AI tasks. This supports attention to visible citations and easy verification in product design; it does not establish that faster AI responses caused users to skip verification.

The convenience sample is small and drawn from one class, and falls below the planned 24–32 participants. Time limits censor completion times, so unrestricted completion-time differences cannot be inferred. The supplied report does not include raw data, realised sequence counts, inter-rater agreement, order-effect analyses or coded interview findings. These should not be presented as completed results.

11 · ETHICS & PRIVACY

Voluntary participation and responsible data handling

Voluntary participation

Participants may decline or stop without penalty and without impact on grades, relationships with staff, or access to university services. Recruitment should avoid pressure from classmates or anyone with assessment authority.

Data minimisation

Use anonymous participant IDs. Avoid collecting names, student IDs, account credentials, private conversations, or other unnecessary personal data.

Secure storage

Limit access to authorised research team members. Before data collection, specify storage location, retention period, deletion procedure, withdrawal deadline, and contact details.

AI answer limitations

AI answers may be inaccurate or incomplete. They are research materials, not authoritative guidance for real administrative decisions; official university sources take precedence.

Recording consent

Screen activity or interview audio should be recorded only after the participant receives an explanation and gives separate consent where required.

Ethical review

Complete applicable university ethics and data-protection procedures before implementation. Do not claim approval has been granted until it has been confirmed.

The consent form should state the study purpose, activities, estimated duration, risks, data collection and use, withdrawal conditions, researcher contact, and optional consent for audio or screen recording. Results should be reported using aggregated statistics and anonymised quotations.
