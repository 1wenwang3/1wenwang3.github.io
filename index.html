HCI RESEARCH PROJECT · XJTLU

Evaluating eHall AI
at XJTLU

Assessing how AI-supported campus information retrieval affects task efficiency, answer quality, trust, satisfaction, and students’ ability to verify and recover from incorrect information.

Explore the study View the procedure

Target group: 92 first-year postgraduate students in AIAT Class 1. Participation and final sample size will depend on voluntary recruitment.

01 · RESEARCH MOTIVATION

Why evaluate eHall AI?

XJTLU’s eHall is an integrated online portal for university information, services, applications, schedules, tasks, search, and other campus resources. Its AI Q&A function provides a natural-language route for students seeking answers, service links, or explanations of administrative procedures.

The study compares traditional information retrieval with AI-assisted retrieval. It examines not only whether AI helps students find information faster, but also whether the answers are accurate, complete, verifiable, and aligned with students’ goals and expectations.

MICRO LEVEL

Task performance

Measure completion time, success rate, answer correctness, and error recovery during specific campus-information tasks.

USER EXPERIENCE

Trust and satisfaction

Understand perceived efficiency, ease of use, confidence, satisfaction, and willingness to use eHall AI again.

MACRO LEVEL

Campus service needs

Explore students’ common information needs, concerns about privacy and reliability, and priorities for future improvement.

Important platform check

The exact capabilities, interface, source citations, and access restrictions of the logged-in eHall AI page must be checked before the experiment. Tasks should be based on services and information that are confirmed to be available; unverified platform features must not be assumed.

02 · RESEARCH QUESTIONS

What do we want to find out?

RQ1 · EFFICIENCY

Does AI save time?

Is eHall AI faster than traditional non-AI information retrieval for comparable campus tasks?

Indicators: completion time, completion rate, first-attempt success.

RQ2 · INFORMATION QUALITY

Can users detect errors?

How accurate, complete, and verifiable are AI answers, and can students identify and correct errors?

Indicators: answer accuracy, error detection, error correction, erroneous trust.

RQ3 · EXPERIENCE

Is AI more satisfying?

Does AI improve perceived ease of use, satisfaction, and future use intention, and does it meet expectations?

RQ4 · USER GOALS

How do students interact?

What are students trying to achieve, what help do they expect, and how do they query, verify, follow up, or seek human help?

RQ5 · SERVICE VALUE

Does it meet student needs?

What are the class’s overall attitudes, common use cases, trust concerns, and priorities for improvement?

HYPOTHESES

Test, do not assume

We expect AI may reduce task time and improve perceived efficiency. Effects on accuracy, satisfaction, trust, and task type will be tested rather than treated as established findings.

04 · PILOT STUDY DESIGN

A small-scale test before the main study

This pilot compares eHall AI-assisted retrieval with traditional official-information search at XJTLU’s SIP campus. It checks task difficulty, session flow, and data-recording procedures while collecting preliminary evidence on efficiency, answer correctness, user experience, and natural verification behaviour.

Scope: this section presents the pilot design followed by its experimental results. The subsequent main-study sections describe the broader proposed main study; its additional task types, longer procedure, and inferential analyses are not part of this eight-person pilot.

8 VOLUNTEERS

AIAT First-Year Class 1

We randomly selected eight volunteers from AIAT First-Year Class 1 to participate in the pilot study.

30–35 MINUTES

One session, both methods

Each person completes four different tasks: two with each method. Participation is voluntary and does not affect grades or university services. The study evaluates the retrieval method, not individual ability.

UP TO 32 TASK RECORDS

Preliminary evidence

Record time, content scores, confidence, verification, and experience. Use the findings primarily to improve the protocol, rather than to establish statistical significance or claim that AI is superior.

CONDITION N

Traditional retrieval

Use official university websites, service guidance, and site search. Ordinary search engines may locate official pages, but generated AI summaries, other chatbots, and help from other people are not allowed.

CONDITION AI

eHall AI-assisted retrieval

Start every task by asking eHall AI. Follow-up questions and verification on official webpages are allowed. Other AI tools and help from other people are not allowed. Start a new AI conversation for each task.

Both conditions access the same scope of official information. AI response waiting, follow-up questions, and webpage verification all count towards task time. Participants only look up information and submit written answers: they do not apply for certificates, book rooms, or make phone calls.

Four tasks across two sets

Each set contains one fact-finding task followed by one rules/procedure task, with a maximum of eight minutes per set.

Within each set, fact finding always precedes the procedural task. This pilot therefore does not isolate a causal effect of task type.

Before the first participant

Verify all official answers and archive sources, checking dates, and page copies. Keep task wording and scoring rules consistent throughout the pilot. Rehearse both methods; prepare the information sheet, consent form, background and post-condition questionnaires, answer sheets, and researcher logs. Standardise the computer, browser, and network as far as possible; complete logins before timing starts.

Specify data storage, authorised access, retention, and the withdrawal deadline, and complete the required course research procedures. Close private pages. Obtain separate consent for screen recording; use manual observation if recording is declined.

STANDARDISED TIMING

Time the complete search

Start when the full task is visible and interaction is allowed; stop at the single final answer submission. Include normal page loading and AI waiting. Record actual elapsed time for early abandonment; at the limit, collect the existing answer and mark a timeout.

Log network failures, crashes, durations, and any accidental content hints. The moderator explains operation only and does not suggest keywords, departments, sources, or answers. Never silently remove or adjust technical incidents.

ANSWER SCORING

Three points per task

Award one point per correct predefined element: 3 = fully correct, 1–2 = partially correct, and 0 = no correct elements. Full success means all three points within the time limit. Retain partial scores.

Contradictory alternatives earn no point unless the correct answer is explicitly selected. Verified equivalent wording and valid official alternative contact details may count. Two researchers independently score all 32 answers where available, hiding condition and time where possible; retain initial scores before resolving differences.

Pilot status: the eight-person pilot has been completed. The protocol above describes the intended procedure; the results below document the observed outcomes and implementation differences.

Pilot study results

Eight volunteers from AIAT First-Year Class 1 completed 32 campus-information tasks, with 16 task records per condition. Four participants also took part in short interviews. The following results are descriptive findings from the completed pilot, reported in the supplied eHall AI Pilot Study Data Analysis Report (EN), Version 2; no significance tests were conducted.

EFFICIENCY · MEDIAN TASK TIME

165 s vs 180 s

eHall AI versus traditional search. Six of eight participants recorded a lower total response time with AI; the median paired difference was −86.5 seconds (AI minus traditional).

ACCURACY · MEAN TASK SCORE

2.12 vs 1.44 / 3

eHall AI versus traditional search. Full success—three points within the time limit—was achieved in 7/16 AI tasks and 5/16 traditional-search tasks.

EXPERIENCE · SATISFACTION

4.19 vs 2.44 / 5

eHall AI versus traditional search. AI received higher mean ratings on all four experience items, although its objective performance varied across tasks.

The advantage depends on the task

AI was faster and more accurate on A1 (Academic Affairs information) and B2 (electronic enrolment certificates). Traditional search was faster and more accurate on B1 (library contact channels). A2 (discussion-room rules) had similar response times, with a slightly higher mean score under traditional search. The pilot does not support a universal claim that AI performs better.

What the four interviews explain

Interview coverage: A02, C02, D01, and D02. Themes below paraphrase the report’s anonymised interview findings.

NAVIGATION

Finding an entry point is difficult

Three interviewees identified A1 as the hardest task. Unclear website navigation made official information difficult to locate; AI could bypass some of that navigation effort.

TRUST

Specific answers feel credible

Concrete entry points and exact times often encouraged trust without checking. This offers a possible explanation for high confidence in incomplete AI answers.

VERIFICATION

Checking is often conditional

Some interviewees checked only when information seemed vague; habitual verification was not universal. Source accessibility also affected whether checking was possible.

METHOD PREFERENCE

Familiarity changes the choice

Traditional search could feel more direct when participants already knew where to look. AI appeared more useful when the service route was unfamiliar.

ANSWER COMPLETENESS

Missing details cost points

The report attributes many AI score losses to omitted contact details, booking limits, usage duration, or fee information, rather than clearly incorrect facts.

PRODUCT FEEDBACK

Make answers easier to check

Requested improvements included direct source links, more complete and specific answers, faster responses, and a frequently asked questions list.

What we take forward

The pilot supports the feasibility of the two-condition design and identifies useful task differences. AI improved results on some tasks and received more positive experience ratings, but performed worse on B1. The main study should strengthen recording, questionnaire consistency, answer-completeness assessment, and source-verification measures before drawing broader conclusions.

05 · EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN

Within-subject comparison: No AI vs With AI

Each participant experiences both information-retrieval conditions, allowing each person to serve as their own comparison. To reduce learning and carry-over effects, participants complete parallel task sets of comparable difficulty rather than repeating exactly the same questions.

CONDITION A

No AI

Participants use traditional search, official university webpages, and service pages to locate and evaluate information. They do not use the AI Q&A function.

CONDITION B

With AI

Participants use eHall AI to answer the task and may ask follow-up questions or check official sources within the study rules.

Counterbalancing and task sets

GroupStage 1Stage 2
AB groupNo AI + Task Set AWith AI + Task Set B
BA groupWith AI + Task Set ANo AI + Task Set B

Randomly assign participants to AB or BA, aiming for similar group sizes and stratifying by programme where feasible. Task Sets A and B should cover the same task types at similar difficulty. Pilot testing should be used to adjust task difficulty.

Controlled comparison

Keep task difficulty, answer rubrics, devices, browser, network environment, instructions, time limits, access procedures, and scoring standards as consistent as possible. Record the eHall AI version and any platform or knowledge-base changes during data collection.

07 · TASK DESIGN & ERROR EVALUATION

Realistic, verifiable campus tasks

Each task should have a clear goal, observable actions, and a defensible answer key. The examples below are templates; before the study, the team must confirm the relevant service and official information are currently accessible.

TASK 01

Fact finding

Goal: Find an official service entry point, responsible department, or eligibility condition.

Expected help: A concise and direct answer.

Scoring: Factual correctness, valid source, completion time.

TASK 02

Procedural understanding

Goal: Summarise the steps, required materials, and channel for a verified campus service.

Expected help: Organise dispersed information into clear steps.

Scoring: Completeness and correctness of steps and conditions.

TASK 03

Information synthesis

Goal: Compare details across two official sources and draw a supported conclusion.

Expected help: Summarise key information and make sources traceable.

Scoring: Coverage, conclusion accuracy, source traceability.

TASK 04

Error detection and verification

Goal: Check an answer against authoritative information, identify inaccuracies, and provide a correction.

Expected help: Correct information or a clear statement of uncertainty.

Scoring: Error detection, error correction, and erroneous trust.

USER INTENTION

What are you trying to do?

Before each task, ask what the participant intends to achieve, what help they expect, and what outcome would count as success.

OUTCOME

Did the user succeed?

Record actions, time, final outcome, verification behaviour, and whether the participant accepted incorrect information.

Fair and safe error testing

Build an answer key from current official sources before testing, recording sources and update dates. At least two researchers should independently check key answers. Observe naturally occurring AI errors; if controlled error examples are needed, use clearly labelled research materials in a simulated task. Do not alter the live university system or expose students to false information for real administrative decisions.

Error categories

TypeHow it will be checked
Factual errorCompare service names, eligibility, and other facts with official sources.

11 · ETHICS & PRIVACY

Voluntary participation and responsible data handling

Voluntary participation

Participants may decline or stop without penalty and without impact on grades, relationships with staff, or access to university services. Recruitment should avoid pressure from classmates or anyone with assessment authority.

Data minimisation

Use anonymous participant IDs. Avoid collecting names, student IDs, account credentials, private conversations, or other unnecessary personal data.

Secure storage

Limit access to authorised research team members. Before data collection, specify storage location, retention period, deletion procedure, withdrawal deadline, and contact details.

AI answer limitations

AI answers may be inaccurate or incomplete. They are research materials, not authoritative guidance for real administrative decisions; official university sources take precedence.

Recording consent

Screen activity or interview audio should be recorded only after the participant receives an explanation and gives separate consent where required.

Ethical review

Complete applicable university ethics and data-protection procedures before implementation. Do not claim approval has been granted until it has been confirmed.

The consent form should state the study purpose, activities, estimated duration, risks, data collection and use, withdrawal conditions, researcher contact, and optional consent for audio or screen recording. Results should be reported using aggregated statistics and anonymised quotations.
